# **1. Introduction**

## **Defining Safety**

Safety is not "a state in which no accidents occur," but rather a cyclical process of understanding the Hazards and Risks inherent in experiments, assessing them appropriately, sharing the insights gained, and continuously improving. The WHO's Laboratory Biosafety Manual, 4th Edition (LBM4), similarly adopts a "risk- and evidence-based approach," determining measures based on risk and evidence rather than uniform rules[^1].

A Hazard refers to a factor—such as a biological agent, equipment, or reagent—that is inherently capable of causing harm. Risk, on the other hand, is only determined once the likelihood and consequences of that Hazard are evaluated, taking into account the experimental procedure, working environment, and the operator's skill level. Risk Assessment must therefore evaluate not only the biological agent itself, but also the procedures, environment, and people surrounding it[^1].

However, properly assessing Risk alone is not enough to maintain Safety. Only when minor abnormalities and near-misses are consistently shared—rather than overlooked—and the resulting insights are fed back into subsequent Risk Assessments and daily practice, does Safety continue to improve. We regard the continuous cycle of understanding Hazards/Risks → appropriate assessment → sharing near-misses → continuous improvement as the fundamental principle underlying our approach to Safety.

## **Safety at iGEM-Waseda** 

We put this cycle into practice through the initiatives introduced in the four chapters of this page.

* **Understand Hazards & Risks**: Through SDS sharing and reagent management in Our Laboratory (Chapter 2) and the education and certification system in Training and Certification (Chapter 3), all members correctly understand Hazards and Risks.  
* **Assess Risks Properly**: In Risk Assessment (Chapter 4), Hazards are identified from three perspectives—Physical, Chemical, and Biological—and corresponding countermeasures are implemented.  
* **Near-Miss Sharing**: Through daily records via the post-experiment report in Our Laboratory (Chapter 2) and in-house near-miss case studies used in the Supervisor Certification Test, even minor abnormalities are shared rather than overlooked.  
* **Continuous Improvement**: Through periodic review of the manuals in Emergency Responses (Chapter 5) and ongoing learning via Continuing Education (Chapter 3⑤), the insights gained are reflected in future practice.

In addition, we did not want to keep this cycle within our own team, and wanted to make it easier for other iGEM teams to put safe lab practices into operation as well. We therefore developed and released “Lab Safety Certification,” a general-purpose web-based testing platform. This platform administers tests for wet-lab members to help them acquire the knowledge needed to conduct experiments safely. Because the knowledge required for safety differs from lab to lab, each team can freely design the test content based on its own teaching materials. We also made the platform easy to use even for people without programming knowledge, and durable enough to remain useful over the long term regardless of changing times. Waseda-Tokyo also used this platform to train and certify its wet-lab members.  For details, please see Training and Certification (Chapter 3)「同じページのWebテストの章に飛ばす」 and the Software page「ソフトウェアのページに飛ばす」.

## **Managements**

We are undergraduate students in our first to fourth years, and do not yet have sufficient experience conducting specialized experiments. For this reason, we have implemented several measures to ensure experiments are conducted safely. The content and objectives of these measures are described below. 

1. **Establishment of an Experiment Supervisor**   
   In our team, members are divided into the following three roles according to their degree of involvement in and responsibility for wet-lab experiments: "Non-Participants," who do not take part in wet-lab experiments involving hands-on procedures in the lab; "Participants," who carry out wet-lab experiments as individual members; and "Supervisors," who oversee the wet-lab experiment as a whole and guide each member with appropriate instructions.   
   To move from Non-Participant to Participant, and from Participant to Supervisor, members must each pass a designated test to demonstrate that they possess the knowledge and skills required for that role. Details of this certification system are described in the "3. Training and Certification" section.   
   While the members participating in wet-lab experiments vary from day to day, at least one Supervisor is, in principle, always required to be present whenever an experiment is conducted. This system serves not only to properly manage experimental procedures and ensure wet-lab experiments proceed smoothly and accurately, but also to ensure that, should a safety issue arise during an experiment—such as an accident or equipment malfunction—the situation can be accurately assessed and the necessary response taken promptly.

2. **Post-Experiment Report**   
   As proof that each day's experiment was completed without issue, we fill out a post-experiment report every day. The content of this report is divided into four stages.  
   **I. Recording the Experiment Date and Experimenters' Names**  
   First, we record the date on which the experiment was conducted and the names of the members involved. This allows us to quickly identify which members should be consulted regarding the circumstances at the time, should any problem come to light at a later date. The "problems" referred to here are not limited to experimental results alone; they also include safety-related issues such as equipment abnormalities or damage, as well as problems that may only become apparent later, such as mix-ups of reagents or samples, or deficiencies in storage and management.  
   **II. Listing the Equipment Used**  
   We list all equipment used in that day's experiment. At the end of the experiment, we check whether any issues have arisen with that equipment and note that no problems were found, if that is the case. This makes it easy to quickly check, should a problem later arise with shared equipment, whether we were among the most recent users of it. Recording who used a piece of shared equipment and when is extremely important, and is one of the rules that must always be followed when conducting experiments. Properly checking experimental equipment at the end of use helps protect not only ourselves, but also other experimenters who use the same equipment, from the risk of accidents.  
   **III. Checklist Confirmation**  
   We perform a final check to confirm there are no issues with the equipment used in the experiment or the surrounding area of the lab bench. This check is always carried out personally by the Supervisor.  
   **IV. Instructor's Signature**  
   Once the above entries are complete, the experiment instructor (a member of Professor Asahi's lab, who serves as PI) reviews the content and provides a signature. Only once this signature has been obtained is that day's experiment considered to have been completed without issue. In addition, when receiving the signature, we briefly share the following day's experiment plans, and at this point we also request accompaniment for any equipment that requires it.

3. **Sharing SDS for Hazardous Reagents**   
   Some of the reagents used in our wet-lab experiments carry hazards such as toxicity to the human body or flammability. To handle these reagents safely, we review the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) before use and share its contents among experiment participants. Shared SDS documents are kept accessible so that any member conducting the experiment can refer to them whenever needed. The purpose of sharing SDS data extends beyond simply understanding a reagent's hazards: for each reagent, we confirm in advance the appropriate protective equipment, safe handling procedures, storage conditions, disposal methods, and first-aid measures in the event of a leak or exposure, and reflect these in our actual experimental procedures. When using a particularly hazardous reagent for the first time, we always confirm the handling procedure under the supervision of the Supervisor before beginning the experiment. For example, since 2-mercaptoethanol can cause health effects through inhalation or skin contact, we handle it using appropriate protective equipment based on its SDS. Flammable reagents such as ethanol are kept away from open flames and heat sources and are stored in a designated location separate from general reagents. During storage, we clearly label the reagent name and hazard information, and regularly check that containers remain properly sealed and that nothing is abnormal about the storage location. Used reagents and waste liquids are also sorted and disposed of in accordance with university and laboratory regulations, so that chemically incompatible substances are not inadvertently mixed. In this way, starting from the sharing of SDS data, we carry out a continuous chain of safety management covering pre-use confirmation, proper handling, segregated storage, and disposal of reagents.

## **Waste Disposal**

Waste generated from our experiments is classified as follows, in accordance with the standards established by Waseda University's administrative office[^2]:

**P: Plastic Equipment Waste** (plastic equipment contaminated with chemicals, rubber/silicone equipment, gloves and masks, paper products)

* Examples  
  * Tubes used in experiments  
  * Tips used in experiments  
  * Gloves and Kimwipes contaminated with chemicals  
* Note  
  * Waste contaminated with genetically modified organisms is always autoclaved (A.C.) before disposal

**S2: Organic Solids** (polymer compounds, resins, etc.)

* Examples  
  * Electrophoresis gels

**Ⅱ-h: Organic Liquid Waste Containing 5% or More Water** (excluding halogenated organic liquid waste and liquid waste containing ferrocyanide/ferricyanide compounds or metals)

* Examples  
  * Liquid media  
  * Sterilized bacterial culture

After being disposed of in their respective designated containers, this waste is transported to the research facility's disposal site on a designated day of the week. At that time, we are required to record information on official forms, such as which laboratory the waste originated from and specifically what types of waste are included. Since we borrow a section of the Asahi Laboratory within the Department of Life Science and Medical Bioscience (School of Advanced Science and Engineering), we are always accompanied by a student from the Asahi Laboratory when disposing of waste.

Our team divides members into three categories—"Non-Participant," "Participant," and "Supervisor"—and has established a certification system in which members advance to Participant and then to Supervisor by sequentially passing the steps ①–④ described below. The purpose of this system is twofold: to ensure that all members acquire the necessary safety knowledge, and to clearly identify individuals capable of providing instruction and supervision.

**① Online Seminar**

Members complete the online seminar provided by the university (covering laws and regulations, measures to prevent the spread of organisms, safe working techniques, accident response, and the university's management system). Only those who complete this seminar may proceed to the next step. 

**② Participant Certification Test**  
Using a 10-chapter set of teaching materials created based on the "Safety Handbook (TWIns)"[^3], the "Guide to Using the Environmental Conservation Center"[^2], and the "Standard Protocols (GP01–08)"[^4], among other sources, members learn about laws and regulations and measures to prevent the spread of organisms, the fundamentals and procedures of recombinant DNA experiments, PPE, the safe use of basic techniques and equipment, management of reagents and biological materials, waste segregation, risk assessment, and initial response to accidents. The passing criterion is a perfect score (100%), as we believe there is no acceptable margin for "roughly understanding" safety knowledge. Members who fail review the relevant material and retake the test.

**③ Training**  
Members acquire practical techniques and an awareness of hazards that cannot be learned through knowledge alone, progressing through three stages under the observation of a Supervisor: observation → assisted practice → independent performance. The transition to independent performance occurs once the Supervisor has judged, on three or more occasions, that the member can "perform correctly on their own." Any deviations or mistakes are pointed out on the spot, and the progress of each member's training is recorded and managed in an Excel sheet. 

**④ Supervisor Certification Test**  
Eligibility to take this test requires having completed Training and demonstrating an understanding of the handling and disposal of biological materials and chemicals, SDS, and emergency response. In addition to the scope covered in the Participant test, this test covers SDS and GHS, procedures for final disposal of waste, safety evaluation based on Risk Group[^5], White List[^6], and Red Flag[^7] classifications, considerations for dual-use research, and instructional methods. The case studies use real (anonymized) near-miss incidents that have occurred within the university, assessing not only knowledge but also the ability to exercise sound judgment in the moment. The passing criterion is likewise a perfect score.

## **Lab Safety Certification**

The tests administered in Steps ② and ④ are conducted using “Lab Safety Certification”, a web application that we developed ourselves. This software serves as a platform that enables various iGEM teams to acquire safety knowledge tailored to their own labs. By using this software, members of other iGEM teams can also efficiently acquire safety knowledge, while instantly grasping how well each member has retained that knowledge. This, in turn, enables other iGEM teams to reduce risk in their own labs and conduct experiments safely.

Previously, Waseda-Tokyo administered certification tests for wet-lab members on paper. However, this presented several challenges: both administrators and test-takers had to coordinate schedules to take the test, grading and test creation were time-consuming, and incorrect answers could not be reviewed afterward. By moving to a web application, test-takers can now take the test at any time, and administrators can instantly check who has taken the test and who has passed. The entire process—from taking the test to grading and review—can also be completed consistently on the web.

The need for safety education and certification testing is not unique to our team; it is likely a common challenge shared by iGEM teams in general. At the same time, because each team's lab rules and the organisms and equipment they use differ, the same set of questions cannot simply be reused as-is. We therefore developed and released this software as a platform on which the content of the questions can be freely designed by each team, while the infrastructure for taking tests, grading, and tracking progress is shared in common.

### **Design Philosophy of Lab Safety Certification** 

The need to reduce risk in the laboratory and conduct experiments safely remains constant regardless of the era. At the same time, what must be done to ensure safety differs slightly from one lab to another. For this reason, we developed this software with the aim of making it easy for other iGEM teams to use and keeping it in use over the long term.

For question creation in particular, rather than building AI directly into the software, we designed the system so that questions are either generated with an external AI service (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, etc.) based on the teaching materials created in Steps ② and ④, or written manually. We also considered embedding AI into the software via an API; however, this approach is vulnerable to changes in AI specifications, and keeping up with those changes would require programming knowledge. We therefore designed question creation to rely on an external AI service or manual writing, based on educational materials.

### **Features of Lab Safety Certification** 

Administrators of the certification test can do the following:

1. Create a test using either questions generated with an external AI service (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, etc.) based on the teaching materials created in Steps ② and ④, or questions created manually  
2. Check each member's test results  
3. Check each member's role

Test-takers can do the following:

1. View the teaching materials created in Steps ② and ④  
2. Take the test  
3. View their score immediately, along with explanations  
4. View teaching materials for review

## **Tips for Creating Teaching Materials and Questions**

To use Lab Safety Certification, each team needs to prepare teaching materials based on its own lab’s rules. Here we share how we created our materials and questions, along with precautions for using AI services, as a reference for other teams.

### **Creating Teaching Materials**

**1. Collect source materials**  
 Gather the documents your lab actually follows, such as publicly available safety handbooks and laws from the government, your university, and the facilities you use, your lab regulations, your team’s protocols, and the SDSs of the reagents you use.

**2. Decide the chapter structure**  
 Organize the content into the topics your lab needs, such as laws and containment measures, experimental procedures, PPE, equipment use, management of reagents and biological materials, waste disposal, risk assessment, and initial response to accidents. Our teaching materials for Participants consist of 10 chapters covering topics like these. While referring to the materials you have collected, include safety management methods specific to how your own lab operates.

**3. Write in your own words**  
 Rather than copying the source materials, write down the operating methods that are best suited to your own lab.

**4. Create questions from the teaching materials**  
 Enter the prompt that can be copied from the Lab Safety Certification admin screen, together with the materials you created, into any external AI service to generate questions, or write the questions yourself. At Waseda-Tokyo, we created 60 questions and had 30 of them selected at random for each test, so we used an external AI (Claude) to generate questions easily. We then entered the questions the AI output in JSON format into the Lab Safety Certification admin screen, and always checked every question and answer choice. Where necessary, we revised the questions themselves or edited the answer choices.

When entering teaching materials into an external AI service, please keep the following in mind.

* **Do not include confidential information.** Do not enter unpublished research content, intellectual property such as sequences, or information covered by agreements with collaborators or companies.  
* **Do not include personal information.** Do not enter names, student ID numbers, or contact details. Anonymize accident and near-miss cases by removing anything that could identify individuals or dates.  
* **Check your institution’s rules.** Confirm that your institution does not restrict entering such materials into external services.  
* **Always verify the generated content.** AI output may contain errors. Check safety-related content in particular against the original sources.

## **③Biological Risks**

As a biological risk, we first assessed the safety of the chassis organism itself. In this project, we used only three strains of *Escherichia coli* as chassis: DH5α, Top10, and BL21(DE3). DH5α and Top10 are laboratory strains derived from the K-12 lineage, and both are classified as Risk Group 1 (microorganisms that do not cause disease in healthy adults) under iGEM's Risk Group guidance. BL21(DE3) is a laboratory strain derived from the B lineage, but is likewise non-pathogenic and treated as Risk Group 1. All three strains are standard laboratory strains listed on the iGEM White List.

Please note that this chapter addresses the general risks associated with laboratory work and facility operation. CPI (Cargo Protein Injection) and TPD (Targeted Protein Degradation), the proteins used in our experiments, have project-specific functions, so risks such as target specificity, off-target effects, behavior in the environment, and quality control of the final formulation must be evaluated individually. These risks are addressed on the CPI Safety「CPIのSafetyに飛ばす」 and TPD Safety 「TPDのSafetyに飛ばす」pages, following the Progressive Safety Assessment Framework (PSAF)「PSAFに飛ばす」.

In addition, we implemented the following countermeasures to address operational risks such as environmental release of recombinant organisms, gene dissemination, and mix-ups (Table 11.4.4).

# **5. Emergency Responses**

During an accident in the lab, the outcome can depend heavily on the actions taken in the first few minutes immediately afterward. For this reason, we created emergency response manuals addressing four scenarios: "Injury," "Earthquake," "Fire," and "Environmental Release." Their content is based on the Safety Handbook of TWIns, our site of activity[^3], and the guide from Waseda University's Environmental Conservation Center[^2]. Procedures for first aid and initial fire suppression were confirmed against official materials from organizations such as the Japanese Red Cross Society[^9] and fire authorities[^10]. We also incorporated responses tailored to our specific experimental context (BSL-1, *E. coli*, liquid nitrogen), such as the procedure for handling a spilled bacterial culture.

The manual consists of five Yes/No flowcharts along with detailed pages. By answering "Yes" or "No" at each step, anyone can reach the appropriate next action without hesitation. The flowcharts are also linked to one another—for example, if a fire breaks out following an earthquake, the user can move on to the corresponding flow. This manual is posted in the laboratory and reviewed once a year, as well as whenever team membership changes.
