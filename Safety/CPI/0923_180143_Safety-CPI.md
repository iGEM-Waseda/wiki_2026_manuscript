# 1. Overview

Cargo Protein Injection (CPI) is the protein-delivery module of SUNRISE. It is designed to recognize a target bacterium, physically penetrate the cell envelope, and deliver a protein payload directly into the target cell. CPI is based on the Photorhabdus virulence cassette (PVC), an extracellular contractile injection system (eCIS). PVC is a phage-tail-like protein complex containing a contractile sheath, an inner tube, a spike/baseplate region, and receptor-recognition elements; contraction of the sheath drives the tube–spike structure toward the target membrane and enables protein delivery.[^1][^2][^3][^14]

In SUNRISE, the **final CPI delivery particle is designed as an all-protein, non-replicating delivery system that does not carry an encoding genome**. This statement applies to the purified delivery particle, not to the production process. CPI is produced using recombinant *Escherichia coli* and recombinant plasmid DNA, so producer cells, residual DNA, and other process-derived materials must still be controlled during research and future manufacturing. In addition, the absence of a packaged genome does not by itself establish Safety. Off-target binding, unintended injection, membrane damage, payload-dependent effects, immune responses, and manufacturing-derived impurities must be evaluated separately.[^1][^3]

PVC also provides a modular basis for changing both the delivered protein and the targeting element. Kreitz et al. demonstrated that non-native proteins, including Cre recombinase, can be loaded using the N-terminal packaging domain of Pdp1, and that engineering the PVC tail fiber can alter tropism in mammalian systems.[^1] Independent work also showed that N-terminal signal peptides derived from PVC effectors can support loading of diverse heterologous proteins into PVC particles.[^17] However, published data do not establish that a gp17-modified PVC will necessarily bind to or inject proteins into bacteria. Bacteriophage T7 gp17 is known to participate in the initial recognition of *E. coli* lipopolysaccharide (LPS), but the performance and specificity of a gp17-derived recognition element after incorporation into our CPI architecture must be validated experimentally.[^5]

During the research stage, CPI is therefore evaluated not only as a purified protein particle but also together with the recombinant organisms, recombinant DNA, experimental payloads, and target bacteria used to produce and test it. For future implementation, Safety must additionally be considered across manufacturing, purification, storage, transport, administration, and disposal.

To organize these questions systematically, we applied our **Progressive Safety Assessment Framework (PSAF)** to CPI. PSAF evaluates Safety across four stages: Contained Research, Unintended Exposure, Intended Use, and Life-cycle & Deployment. The design principles and evaluation procedure of PSAF are described on the [PSAF Design] page. This page focuses on CPI-specific Risks. General recombinant-DNA laboratory management is addressed in [General Laboratory Safety], while Safety issues intrinsic to the therapeutic degradation payload are addressed in [TPD Safety].

# 2. Risk Assessment

## 2.1 Stage 1 — Contained Research Safety

Stage 1 evaluates Risks that arise while CPI research is conducted in a controlled laboratory environment.

### 2.1.1 Risk Exploration

Risk Scenarios were explored from the Biological Components, Engineered Function, and Process / Use of the current CPI research system.

:::c
**Table 2. Risk Exploration for Stage 1 — Contained Research Safety**
:::

| **Hazard Source** | **CPI-specific items** |
| --- | --- |
| **Biological Components** | Tail fiber / receptor-recognition element, spike and inner tube, payload, PVC-associated genes, expression plasmids, recombinant producer *E. coli*, target bacteria |
| **Engineered Function** | Cell-surface recognition, membrane penetration, protein injection, Cre/loxP recombination in the proof-of-concept assay |
| **Process / Use** | Cloning, culture, cell disruption, particle purification, delivery assay, storage, transport within the laboratory, and waste disposal |

Five principal Risk Scenarios were identified and are assessed below.

### 2.1.2 Risk Assessment

:::details Risk 1. Donor-derived biological hazards
#### Hazardous Property
PVC is derived from *Photorhabdus asymbiotica*. Although *Photorhabdus* species are best known as insect-associated bacteria, *P. asymbiotica* has also been reported as a human pathogen.[^4] Native PVC systems can carry toxic effectors such as Pdp1 and Pnf. Pdp1 perturbs dNTP pools, whereas Pnf affects the actin cytoskeleton when delivered into mammalian macrophages.[^2] Earlier work also established injectable insecticidal activity of recombinant PVC preparations in an insect model.[^14]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
During cloning, expression, cell disruption, purification, and delivery assays, researchers may be exposed to recombinant producer cells, cell lysates, or purified CPI preparations. The relevant Hazard is not the taxonomic origin of the genes alone, but whether the engineered construct retains or introduces a biological function capable of causing harm.

#### Consequence
If native toxic effectors or other harmful *Photorhabdus*-derived functions were retained in the delivery system, exposure could create payload-dependent cytotoxicity or other unintended biological effects.

#### Control
Our CPI design does not use the native toxic effectors Pdp1 or Pnf as therapeutic payloads. Instead, the proof-of-concept delivery assay uses a non-native payload, Cre recombinase, loaded through a Pdp1-derived N-terminal packaging sequence. Any future payload is evaluated independently for its own biological activity. The construct should also be checked to confirm that no unintended native effector coding sequences are retained in the production configuration.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Jiang et al. directly demonstrated that Pdp1 and Pnf are PVC cargoes capable of killing mammalian macrophages after delivery.[^2] Kreitz et al. demonstrated that the Pdp1 N-terminal packaging domain can be used to load non-native proteins into PVC particles.[^1] Removing native toxic effectors reduces one defined Hazard pathway, but it does not demonstrate that the remaining CPI particle or a replacement payload is intrinsically safe. Each design change therefore requires reassessment.
:::

:::details Risk 2. Researcher exposure to functional CPI particles
#### Hazardous Property
Purified CPI particles retain a mechanically active contractile injection apparatus. Even without native toxic effectors, a functional particle may bind to a cell, contract, penetrate a membrane, and deliver its payload if the necessary targeting and activation conditions are met.[^1][^3]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Researchers may encounter purified particles during concentration, ultracentrifugation, resuspension, transfer, spill cleanup, or other handling steps. Exposure may occur through contact with skin or mucosal surfaces or through aerosols generated by laboratory procedures.

#### Consequence
A harmful outcome would require a sequence of events: binding to a human cell, productive activation of the injection apparatus, and delivery of a payload capable of producing a biological effect. These steps should be assessed separately rather than assuming that the presence of a functional PVC particle alone is equivalent to toxicity.

#### Control
The current bacterial-targeting design uses a gp17-derived recognition element because T7 gp17 participates in recognition of *E. coli* LPS.[^5] Computational off-target analysis can be used as an initial screen, but it is not treated as proof of non-binding. Purified CPI is handled as a biologically active protein complex, and future biological use requires direct tests of binding, delivery, and cytotoxicity in relevant human cell types. Spill, aerosol, and ultracentrifugation controls are addressed in [General Laboratory Safety].

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Kreitz et al. showed that engineered PVC activity in several mammalian models can depend strongly on receptor-binding interactions, but they also observed Pvc13-independent activity in J774A.1 macrophages, with the spike component Pvc10 contributing to activity in that context.[^1] Therefore, Safety assessment should not assume that tail-fiber specificity alone completely determines mammalian off-target activity. The gp17-based bacterial-targeting configuration has not yet been demonstrated in the literature to provide productive bacterial injection by PVC, so its specificity and activity must be established experimentally.
:::

:::details Risk 3. Recombinant producer strain and recombinant DNA
#### Hazardous Property
CPI production requires a viable recombinant *E. coli* producer containing plasmid DNA encoding the CPI system. The purified CPI particle is intended to be non-replicating, but the producer organism and recombinant DNA used to manufacture it are biologically and legally distinct materials.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Producer cells or recombinant DNA could leave containment through spills, aerosols, contaminated instruments, culture waste, or inadequately purified preparations. Residual producer cells or nucleic acids in a nominally protein-only preparation would also invalidate the assumption that the administered material consists only of CPI protein particles.

#### Consequence
Release of viable recombinant bacteria could prolong environmental exposure. Recombinant DNA could also persist outside the intended workflow, although the likelihood of transfer into other organisms depends on the DNA state, vector architecture, recipient organisms, and environmental conditions. It is therefore inappropriate to assume that the PVC construct will automatically spread by conjugation unless a conjugative mechanism is actually present.

#### Control
Research involving recombinant producer organisms is conducted under the containment measures required for second-type use of genetically modified organisms in Japan.[^7][^8] Producer cells and DNA-containing wastes are inactivated before disposal. Purification procedures are designed to separate CPI particles from viable cells and residual nucleic acid. Future product development should include explicit specifications for residual host-cell DNA and viable-cell contamination. Where NEB 10-beta is used as the producer strain, it is a DH10B-derived *E. coli* K-12 laboratory strain.[^6]

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Japanese guidance defines genetically modified organisms on the basis of organisms containing nucleic acid generated through specified recombinant techniques, and requires appropriate containment for second-type use.[^7][^8] This supports treating the recombinant producer separately from the purified CPI particle. The actual residual-cell and residual-DNA levels after CPI purification have not yet been established and must be measured for the finalized process.
:::

:::details Risk 4. Nucleic-acid-modifying proof-of-concept payload
#### Hazardous Property
The current proof-of-concept payload is Cre recombinase. Cre catalyzes site-specific recombination between loxP sites,[^15] so it is a nucleic-acid-modifying protein even though CPI delivers it as a protein rather than as a nucleic acid. Kreitz et al. similarly used Pdp1-NTD–Cre to demonstrate functional PVC-mediated delivery.[^1]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
In the current assay, Cre is delivered into a reporter bacterium containing an engineered loxP reporter. If a nucleic-acid-modifying payload were delivered to other cells or organisms, its biological consequences would depend on the presence of compatible target sequences and on the payload's activity.

#### Consequence
Within the current reporter strain, recombination is the intended readout. In a different biological context, a nucleic-acid-modifying payload could generate unintended genetic changes. This Risk is payload-specific and should not be generalized to the future therapeutic TPD adaptor, which is designed to act at the protein level.

#### Control
Cre/loxP experiments are restricted to contained laboratory assays using engineered reporter strains. Cre is treated as a proof-of-concept delivery payload rather than as the intended therapeutic payload of SUNRISE. Any future use of a nucleic-acid-modifying payload would trigger a separate Safety and regulatory assessment.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
The current reporter configuration provides a controlled means to test functional delivery, but it does not establish the Safety of delivering Cre or other genome-modifying proteins into wild-type organisms. Regulatory classification would have to be reconsidered if the payload or recipient context changes.
:::

:::details Risk 5. Target bacteria used for delivery assays
#### Hazardous Property
CPI delivery assays use living bacterial target cells. The Hazard associated with the target organism depends on the strain used, including its pathogenicity, antibiotic-resistance phenotype, and environmental persistence.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Researchers are exposed to target bacteria during culture, particle-incubation experiments, sampling, and disposal.

#### Consequence
Use of pathogenic or clinically derived strains could create infection or environmental Risks independent of the CPI particle itself.

#### Control
Current proof-of-concept delivery assays are restricted to laboratory *E. coli* strains under contained *in vitro* conditions. Expansion to clinical isolates, multidrug-resistant strains, or higher-risk organisms would require strain-specific containment and Safety review before experimentation.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Safety conclusions from laboratory strains cannot be extrapolated automatically to clinical isolates. Strain-specific characteristics and the applicable biosafety level must be reassessed whenever the target organism changes.
:::


### 2.1.3 Stage 1 Summary

Stage 1 identified five principal Risk areas: donor-derived biological functions, researcher exposure to functional CPI particles, recombinant producer organisms and DNA, the nucleic-acid-modifying proof-of-concept payload, and the target bacteria used in delivery assays.

Several design choices reduce specific Risks. Native toxic PVC effectors are not used as the therapeutic payload, current assays are performed under containment, and laboratory strains are used for proof-of-concept experiments. However, these controls do not establish the overall Safety of CPI. In particular, the human-cell interaction of the gp17-modified CPI, the purity of CPI preparations, and the effects of future payload changes remain open questions.

Stage 2 therefore extends the assessment to situations in which CPI-related Materials unintentionally leave containment.

## 2.2 Stage 2 — Unintended Exposure Safety

Stage 2 evaluates what could occur if CPI-related Materials unintentionally leave laboratory containment.

### 2.2.1 Risk Exploration

The analysis distinguishes between release of viable recombinant organisms or recombinant DNA and release of purified CPI particles. For purified particles, environmental arrival is further separated from productive off-target binding, membrane penetration, and payload action.

:::c
**Table 3. Risk Exploration for Stage 2 — Unintended Exposure Safety**
:::

| **Hazard Source** | **CPI-specific items** |
| --- | --- |
| **Biological Components** | Recombinant producer cells, recombinant DNA, purified CPI particles, receptor-recognition element, spike/inner tube, payload |
| **Engineered Function** | Target recognition, membrane penetration, protein injection, payload-dependent intracellular activity |
| **Process / Use** | Spills of cultures or purified particles, contaminated equipment, waste handling, storage, and transport |

Four principal Risk Scenarios were identified and are assessed below.

### 2.2.2 Risk Assessment

:::details Risk 1. Recombinant organisms and DNA outside containment
#### Hazardous Property
The recombinant producer is capable of growth and carries engineered DNA. Residual producer cells or recombinant DNA may also remain in incompletely purified CPI preparations.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Release could occur through spilled culture, lysate, inadequately treated waste, contaminated equipment, or incompletely purified material.

#### Consequence
Viable producer cells could persist long enough to extend exposure. Recombinant DNA could also enter the environment. Horizontal transfer is possible only under suitable biological conditions and should be assessed according to the actual vector and recipient context rather than assumed to occur automatically.

#### Control
Producer-cell processes remain under recombinant-organism containment, and cultures, lysates, and contaminated wastes are inactivated before disposal in accordance with laboratory procedures and Japanese requirements for second-type use.[^7][^8] Purification should include verification that viable producer cells and residual DNA have been adequately removed.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Containment reduces the probability of release but does not substitute for process-specific evidence. Residual viable cells and residual DNA must be measured once the production and purification process is finalized.
:::

:::details Risk 2. Environmental persistence of CPI particles
#### Hazardous Property
A released CPI particle could remain biologically active for some period after leaving containment. Unlike a bacteriophage, however, the intended final CPI particle is a protein complex and does not contain an encoding genome that would allow the particle itself to replicate or autonomously produce progeny.[^1][^3]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Purified particles could reach water, surfaces, waste streams, or other environments after a spill or inadequate waste treatment.

#### Consequence
If particles remain active, the duration of exposure to environmental bacteria or other organisms increases. Actual harm would still depend on particle concentration, persistence, target recognition, productive injection, and payload activity.

#### Control
The non-replicating nature of the final particle prevents self-amplification as a route of exposure escalation. Waste procedures should nevertheless inactivate functional protein particles rather than relying only on their inability to replicate.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Published studies characterize PVC structure and function, but they do not establish a general environmental half-life for engineered CPI particles. Stability in water, on surfaces, in biological fluids, and under disposal conditions remains unknown and should be measured for the final formulation.
:::

:::details Risk 3. Off-target binding to non-target bacteria
#### Hazardous Property
The receptor-recognition element may bind surface structures on bacteria other than the intended target. T7 gp17 participates in recognition of *E. coli* LPS, and host-range changes can be associated with mutations in receptor-binding regions of phage tail fibers.[^5][^16] This indicates that receptor recognition is strain-dependent rather than universally specific.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Released CPI particles could contact environmental bacteria or commensal bacteria that display surface structures sufficiently similar to those of the intended target.

#### Consequence
Off-target binding could create the first step toward unintended injection. If productive delivery also occurs, non-target bacterial physiology or community composition could be altered depending on the payload.

#### Control
Dry-lab interaction analysis is used as a screening layer to compare intended and representative non-target surfaces. Because molecular docking or structural prediction does not directly measure cell binding, candidates with plausible off-target interactions should be prioritized for experimental binding and delivery assays. Future Intended Uses should define the relevant non-target bacterial panel in advance.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Published data support the role of receptor-binding proteins in target recognition, but no finite panel can demonstrate the absence of binding to all untested bacterial strains. In addition, the ability of the gp17-modified CPI to perform productive bacterial injection remains an experimental question. Therefore, computational specificity should be interpreted as comparative evidence, not as proof of Safety.
:::

:::details Risk 4. Membrane damage and payload injection after off-target binding
#### Hazardous Property
CPI contains a contractile membrane-penetrating apparatus. If an off-target interaction becomes productive, both mechanical penetration and payload activity may affect the non-target cell.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
This Scenario requires more than environmental contact: the particle must bind, become productively activated, penetrate the cell envelope, and deliver a biologically active payload.

#### Consequence
Possible Consequences include membrane-associated stress or damage and payload-dependent changes in cell function. These effects should be separated experimentally because a harmful phenotype could originate from either the injection machinery or the payload.

#### Control
Native toxic effectors Pdp1 and Pnf are not used as the therapeutic payload. Delivery specificity and payload Safety are evaluated as separate control layers. Experiments should compare unloaded or non-functional-payload particles with active-payload particles wherever possible so that mechanical and payload-dependent effects can be distinguished.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Kreitz et al. reported no significant cell death above background in primary neurons exposed to Cre-loaded PVCs under their specific experimental conditions, and unloaded PVCs were insufficient to reproduce toxin-dependent cytotoxicity in insect cells.[^1] These findings do not establish that membrane penetration is harmless in all cell types. In particular, the physiological effect of productive CPI penetration in bacteria has not yet been established.
:::


### 2.2.3 Stage 2 Summary

Stage 2 separates two fundamentally different release problems: release of viable recombinant producer organisms or DNA, and release of purified non-replicating CPI particles. For the producer system, the primary Controls are containment, inactivation, and purification. For purified CPI, the principal Safety questions are environmental persistence, off-target recognition, productive injection, and payload activity.

The non-replicating protein-particle design removes one important pathway for exposure escalation: the purified CPI particle cannot amplify itself by replicating a packaged genome. However, non-replication does not determine how long a particle remains active or whether it can act on a non-target organism. Those questions remain empirical.

Stage 3 therefore examines CPI under intentional exposure conditions corresponding to a future therapeutic use.

## 2.3 Stage 3 — Intended-Use Safety

Stage 3 evaluates Risks that could arise if CPI were intentionally administered for treatment of a bacterial infection. The current project is limited to contained *in vitro* research; this Stage does not claim that clinical Safety has been demonstrated.

The organization of future nonclinical questions is informed by the general ICH S6(R1) principle that biological activity, exposure, toxicity, immunogenicity, and dosing strategy should be considered together for biotechnology-derived pharmaceuticals.[^9] CPI is a distinct platform, so the exact regulatory applicability and test design would need to be determined for the final product.

### 2.3.1 Risk Exploration

:::c
**Table 4. Risk Exploration for Stage 3 — Intended-Use Safety**
:::

| **Hazard Source** | **CPI-specific items** |
| --- | --- |
| **Biological Components** | CPI particle, receptor-recognition element, spike/inner tube, therapeutic payload |
| **Engineered Function** | Recognition of the target bacterium, membrane penetration, protein delivery, payload action |
| **Process / Use** | Indication, bacterial strain, administration site, route, dose, duration, and repeat dosing |

Five principal Risk Scenarios were identified and are assessed below.

### 2.3.2 Risk Assessment

:::details Risk 1. Off-target interaction with human cells and tissues
#### Hazardous Property
A bacterial-targeting recognition element may still interact unexpectedly with human cells, and PVC activity may not be determined exclusively by the engineered tail fiber. Kreitz et al. showed strong receptor-dependent targeting in several engineered mammalian systems, but also observed Pvc13-independent activity in J774A.1 macrophages in which the spike component Pvc10 contributed to activity.[^1]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
At the administration site, CPI would contact both bacteria and surrounding human tissues. Any systemic migration would expose additional cell types and organs.

#### Consequence
If binding, membrane penetration, or payload delivery occurred in human cells, cellular dysfunction or tissue injury could result. The magnitude of harm would depend on cell type, dose, distribution, and payload function.

#### Control
Human off-target Safety is therefore evaluated at multiple levels. Dry-lab analysis can screen interactions between the bacterial-targeting recognition element and representative human surface molecules, but experimental testing must evaluate whole-particle binding, delivery, and cytotoxicity in relevant human cells. The spike and other structural components should be included in this assessment rather than assuming that tail-fiber specificity is the only determinant.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Kreitz et al. demonstrated that engineered PVCs can be deliberately retargeted to human receptors and can deliver functional protein cargoes into human cells.[^1] This is evidence of the platform's programmability, but it also establishes that human-cell targeting is biologically possible when compatible interactions exist. Therefore, absence of off-target activity must be demonstrated for the actual CPI design rather than inferred from bacterial intent alone.
:::

:::details Risk 2. Disruption of the commensal microbiome
#### Hazardous Property
A recognition element selected for a pathogen may also bind commensal bacteria that share similar cell-surface structures. Target binding and microbiome sparing are therefore separate properties.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Even with local administration, resident microbiota at or near the infected site may be exposed to CPI. Migration or excretion of particles could expose microbial communities outside the original treatment site.

#### Consequence
Productive delivery into non-target commensals could alter their abundance or function. At the community level, this could change microbiome composition or ecological interactions.

#### Control
SUNRISE uses two conceptually independent specificity layers. CPI provides **Delivery Specificity** at the cell-recognition level, while TPD provides **Molecular Specificity** through recognition of a defined intracellular protein target. This layered design may reduce the probability that an off-target bacterium experiences the intended molecular effect, but it does not eliminate the need to test representative commensal species.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
The two-layer specificity concept is a design rationale, not proof that the microbiome will be unaffected. The relevant commensal panel should be defined for each Intended Use, and future experiments should evaluate both single-species off-target effects and, where appropriate, community-level changes.
:::

:::details Risk 3. Route-dependent exposure and immune responses
#### Hazardous Property
The tissue distribution, local concentration, residence time, and immune exposure of a protein nanoparticle-like complex depend strongly on the administration route. CPI is also composed of multiple bacterial proteins that may be recognized by the host immune system.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Systemic administration would expose a wide range of tissues, whereas local administration is intended to limit exposure primarily to the infected site. Local administration does not completely exclude systemic migration or immune recognition.

#### Consequence
Potential Consequences include off-target tissue exposure, local tissue injury, innate or adaptive immune responses, altered pharmacokinetics, and reduced delivery efficiency after repeated administration.

#### Control
For an initial Intended Use, we prioritize infections that can be accessed locally, such as lower urinary tract or wound infections, rather than assuming systemic administration. The purpose of this choice is to constrain the initial exposure Scenario, not to claim that local administration is already proven safe. Future studies should quantify local retention, systemic distribution, clearance, and immune responses.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
In the mouse experiments of Kreitz et al., intracranially administered PVCs did not produce significant immune-cell activation, inflammatory cytokine production, body-weight loss, or cellular toxicity during the reported experimental time course, and intact particles were not readily detected in brain tissue at day 7.[^1] These results are specific to the tested particle, route, species, dose, and observation period. They do not establish human Safety, systemic Safety, or Safety after repeated administration.
:::

:::details Risk 4. Dose-dependent exposure and repeated administration
#### Hazardous Property
CPI activity depends on more than the mass of total protein. The biologically relevant dose is influenced by the number of intact functional particles, the amount of payload loaded per particle, binding efficiency, injection efficiency, and payload potency. Repeated administration can additionally change immune exposure.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
If delivery efficiency is low, a higher particle dose or repeated administration may be required to obtain a therapeutic effect.

#### Consequence
Increasing dose can increase off-target exposure, local tissue exposure, immune stimulation, and exposure to manufacturing-derived impurities. Repeated administration may alter both Safety and efficacy if anti-CPI immune responses develop.

#### Control
Future nonclinical development should establish a dose–response relationship linking administered material to functional delivery, therapeutic effect, and adverse effects. The minimum effective dose and an appropriate Safety margin should be defined. Repeat-dose studies should evaluate whether prior exposure changes distribution, immunogenicity, or delivery activity.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
ICH S6(R1) emphasizes integrating biological activity, exposure, toxicity, immunogenicity, and dosing strategy when planning nonclinical evaluation of biotechnology-derived products.[^9] For CPI, the relevant dose metric has not yet been validated, and the current Cre/loxP reporter is a functional delivery readout rather than a direct count of successful injection events.
:::

:::details Risk 5. Clinical strain variability and expansion of the recognition-element library
#### Hazardous Property
Surface structures vary among strains within a bacterial species. A recognition element that performs well with a laboratory strain may therefore bind poorly to a clinical isolate or may acquire a different off-target profile. T7 gp17 itself illustrates that host range can be influenced by sequence variation in receptor-binding regions,[^5] while structure-informed engineering of phage tail fibers has independently demonstrated that tail-fiber sequence changes can alter bacterial host range.[^16]

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
This Risk arises when laboratory-strain data are extrapolated to clinical isolates or when a new recognition element is introduced without a corresponding Safety reassessment.

#### Consequence
Insufficient recognition could lead to treatment failure, whereas a broader or altered recognition profile could create new off-target interactions with commensal bacteria or human cells.

#### Control
Future application should confirm the causative strain and demonstrate that the selected CPI recognition element is compatible with that strain. Each newly introduced recognition element is treated as a design change requiring renewed assessment of target binding, non-target binding, productive injection, and whole-particle off-target effects.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
The literature supports receptor-binding proteins as important determinants of host range, but it does not justify transferring specificity data from one engineered tail-fiber configuration to another. Existing evidence can guide screening, but each new configuration requires experimental validation.
:::


### 2.3.3 Stage 3 Summary

Stage 3 identifies human off-target effects, microbiome disruption, route-dependent exposure and immune responses, dose/repeat-dose effects, and clinical-strain variability as the principal Intended-Use Safety questions.

These considerations support an initial development strategy centered on **local administration to accessible infections**, because this can define and constrain the first exposure Scenario. Local administration is not presented as inherently safe; it is a deliberate way to reduce the scope of early exposure while the platform is characterized.

A critical remaining issue is dose. The effective CPI dose and its Safety margin are not yet known. Future studies must connect particle quantity, functional delivery, therapeutic activity, adverse effects, and repeat-dose immunogenicity.

These Safety studies are interpretable only if the material used for testing has defined and consistent quality. Stage 4 therefore addresses manufacturing, quality control, stability, deployment, and information management.

## 2.4 Stage 4 — Life-cycle & Deployment Safety

Stage 4 assumes a future scenario in which CPI is manufactured, purified, stored, transported, administered, and disposed of outside a research-only workflow. The Controls described below include both current design principles and future requirements that would need to be established before deployment.

### 2.4.1 Risk Exploration

:::c
**Table 5. Risk Exploration for Stage 4 — Life-cycle & Deployment Safety**
:::

| **Hazard Source** | **CPI-specific items** |
| --- | --- |
| **Biological Components** | CPI particle, receptor-recognition element, payload, recombinant producer, recombinant DNA, process-derived impurities |
| **Engineered Function** | Target recognition, membrane penetration, injection activity, payload function, interchangeability of modular components |
| **Process / Use** | Scale-up, purification, lot control, storage, transport, administration, disposal, design changes, Material transfer, and information sharing |

Six principal Risk Scenarios were identified and are assessed below.

### 2.4.2 Risk Assessment

:::details Risk 1. Manufacturing-derived impurities and contamination
#### Hazardous Property
Because CPI is produced in *E. coli*, a preparation may contain host-cell proteins, residual host or plasmid DNA, endotoxin, viable-cell contamination, or other process-derived impurities if purification and process controls are insufficient.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Patients could be exposed to these impurities when a CPI preparation is administered. Total exposure depends on both impurity concentration and administered dose.

#### Consequence
Process-derived impurities may contribute to inflammatory or immune responses, while microbial contamination could create infection Risk. Failure to distinguish impurity-driven effects from CPI-driven effects would also confound Safety interpretation.

#### Control
Future manufacturing should define a standardized purification process and product specifications for relevant process-related impurities, including endotoxin, residual DNA, host-cell proteins, and microbial contamination. Acceptance criteria should reflect the route of administration and maximum intended dose. In-process controls should complement final-product testing.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
ICH Q6B provides general principles for specifications, product-related and process-related impurities, biological activity, purity, and reference materials for biotechnological/biological products.[^10] These principles are useful for structuring CPI quality assessment, although the exact regulatory framework and acceptance criteria must be determined for the final product.
:::

:::details Risk 2. Particle state and payload loading
#### Hazardous Property
CPI is a multi-protein complex, so the presence of component proteins alone does not demonstrate that the preparation contains intact, injection-competent particles. Relevant heterogeneity may include incomplete assembly, contraction before use, aggregation, fragmentation, variable payload loading, and free payload.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Two lots with similar total protein concentrations may contain different fractions of intact functional particles or different payload loads.

#### Consequence
Lot-to-lot variation can change the effective delivered dose. Free payload may create an exposure route different from particle-mediated delivery, while inactive or prematurely contracted particles may reduce efficacy and drive dose escalation.

#### Control
Future quality assessment should characterize particle integrity, assembly state, contraction state, aggregation/fragmentation, payload loading, and free payload. Structural and compositional assays should be linked to a functional potency assay rather than interpreted independently.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Kreitz et al. showed that PVC payload loading can be quantified and that different endogenous payloads are loaded at different relative levels.[^1] Jiang et al. independently demonstrated N-terminal-signal-guided loading of diverse heterologous proteins into PVC.[^17] Together, these findings support treating payload loading as a measurable quality attribute. The acceptable range for SUNRISE CPI has not yet been established.
:::

:::details Risk 3. Potency and product stability
#### Hazardous Property
Particle mass or total protein concentration is not equivalent to biological potency. CPI potency depends on intact particle structure, target binding, productive injection, and payload function. These properties may change during storage or transport.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
A product lot may lose activity during storage, freezing/thawing, transport, or handling, or different lots may exhibit different biological activity at the same nominal protein concentration.

#### Consequence
Reduced potency could cause treatment failure and encourage dose escalation, while inconsistent potency would make Safety and efficacy data difficult to compare across studies or lots.

#### Control
A future product should use a standardized functional assay and a characterized reference preparation to express relative potency. The Cre/loxP assay is useful as a proof-of-concept functional-delivery assay, but it should not be equated directly with the number of injection events or with therapeutic TPD activity. Stability studies should monitor identity, integrity, purity, and potency under proposed storage and transport conditions.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
ICH Q6B distinguishes biological activity/potency from quantity and supports use of appropriate reference materials and biological assays.[^10] ICH Q5C provides principles for stability testing of biotechnological/biological products.[^11] A CPI-specific potency assay, reference standard, shelf life, and transport stability specification remain to be established.
:::

:::details Risk 4. Regulatory classification and design changes
#### Hazardous Property
CPI development involves distinct regulated objects: recombinant producer organisms, recombinant DNA, a purified protein delivery particle, the payload, and the biological state of the recipient after delivery. Changing the recognition element, payload, manufacturing process, route, or Intended Use may change the relevant Safety questions and regulatory requirements.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Risk arises when research-stage assumptions are transferred directly to manufacturing or clinical use, or when a modified CPI is treated as equivalent to a previous design without evaluating the change.

#### Consequence
Necessary containment, quality, nonclinical, clinical, or regulatory requirements could be overlooked. In particular, describing CPI as 'non-replicating' or 'all-protein' does not remove the need to manage the recombinant production phase or to assess the final product according to its actual composition and Intended Use.

#### Control
Producer organisms and purified CPI are treated as separate Materials. The applicability of the Cartagena Act and other requirements should be determined from the legal definitions and the actual Material being handled.[^7][^8] For future medical use, product composition, mechanism, route, and indication should be defined before consultation with the appropriate regulatory authority. Design or manufacturing changes should trigger a documented comparability assessment rather than automatic carryover of previous Safety conclusions.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
ICH Q5E provides principles for evaluating comparability before and after manufacturing-process changes in biotechnological/biological products.[^12] These principles support change control for CPI, but they do not predetermine the regulatory category of this platform. Final legal and regulatory classification requires product-specific review.
:::

:::details Risk 5. Environmental release across the life cycle
#### Hazardous Property
Scale-up increases the quantity and frequency with which recombinant producer cells, recombinant DNA, and purified CPI particles are handled. Even a non-replicating final particle can create repeated environmental exposure if releases occur repeatedly.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Potential routes include manufacturing spills, process wastewater, transport incidents, unused product, contaminated equipment, and clinical waste. Depending on the Intended Use, excreted or discharged material may also require assessment.

#### Consequence
A larger or repeated release could increase the frequency or geographic extent of the Stage 2 Risks, including exposure of non-target organisms and release of recombinant production Materials.

#### Control
Future deployment should establish process-specific containment, waste inactivation, spill response, and recordkeeping at the actual manufacturing and use scale. Waste containing viable recombinant producers should be distinguished from waste containing purified protein particles. The current Intended Use does not include deliberate environmental release.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Japanese guidance requires containment for second-type use of genetically modified organisms during research and related handling.[^7][^8] Industrial-scale release Scenarios and post-administration environmental fate have not yet been evaluated for CPI and should not be inferred from laboratory-scale controls.
:::

:::details Risk 6. Dual-use and access to Materials and information
#### Hazardous Property
CPI is modular: both target recognition and payload can be changed. This modularity is scientifically useful, but it also means that the capabilities of the platform may change substantially when different recognition elements or payloads are introduced.

#### Exposure / Access Scenario
Access can occur through physical transfer of Materials, provision of detailed technical information, collaborative research, publication, or other forms of technology transfer.

#### Consequence
A modified system could be used for purposes outside the therapeutic goals of SUNRISE. The Risk depends on the specific Material, capability, recipient, and information being transferred; it is not adequately addressed merely by noting that the current design omits native toxic effectors.

#### Control
Material and information transfer should be evaluated according to function, technical detail, recipient, destination, and Intended Use. When appropriate, institutional biosafety, biosecurity, and export-control offices should be consulted before transfer. The assessment should be updated when the recognition element, payload, or disclosed capability changes.

#### Evidence & Uncertainty
Japan's METI guidance for universities and research institutions emphasizes management of sensitive technologies and compliance with security export-control requirements, including technology transfer.[^13] Legal compliance and broader responsible-research judgment are related but distinct; both should be considered when sharing a modular delivery platform.
:::


### 2.4.3 Stage 4 Summary

Stage 4 identifies manufacturing-derived impurities, particle and payload heterogeneity, potency and stability, regulatory classification and change control, life-cycle environmental release, and dual-use/access as the principal deployment-level Safety questions.

A key conclusion is that Safety and efficacy results are interpretable only when the tested CPI material is sufficiently defined. Manufacturing, purification, storage, and transport must preserve the material attributes that were evaluated experimentally.

For this reason, CPI should not be treated as a single invariant platform for Safety purposes. A more appropriate unit of assessment is the specific combination of **recognition element × payload × target × use condition**, together with the manufacturing process that produces that configuration. Changes in any of these dimensions may require renewed Safety assessment.

# 3. Conclusion

Using PSAF, we evaluated CPI across four progressive contexts: contained laboratory research, unintended exposure outside containment, intended therapeutic use, and the complete life cycle from manufacturing to disposal.

The assessment clarified both design advantages and unresolved uncertainties. The final CPI delivery particle is designed as a non-replicating protein complex without a packaged encoding genome, which removes self-replication of the delivery particle as a route of exposure amplification. Native toxic PVC effectors are not used as the therapeutic payload, and SUNRISE adds a second specificity layer through the molecular specificity of TPD. These features reduce defined Risk pathways, but none of them independently demonstrates overall Safety.

At the research stage, the central issues are donor-derived effector functions, exposure to active CPI particles, recombinant producer organisms and DNA, the Cre proof-of-concept payload, and target bacteria. Under unintended-release conditions, the key questions become environmental persistence, off-target bacterial recognition, and whether off-target binding leads to productive injection and payload action. Under Intended Use, the assessment expands to whole-particle interactions with human tissues, microbiome effects, route-dependent distribution and immunogenicity, dose and repeat dosing, and clinical-strain variability. At the deployment stage, product quality, payload loading, potency, stability, change control, environmental release, and responsible management of Materials and information become essential.

The most important scientific limitation is that several properties central to the future therapeutic concept remain unproven for the current bacterial-targeting CPI design. T7 gp17 is a validated phage receptor-recognition protein, but productive targeting and protein injection into bacteria by the gp17-modified PVC must be demonstrated experimentally.[^5] Likewise, computational off-target analysis can prioritize experiments but cannot establish absence of binding or toxicity. The effect of the injection apparatus itself on bacterial physiology, the environmental stability of the engineered particle, its distribution and immune effects after clinically relevant administration, its effective dose, and a CPI-specific potency metric all require additional data.

Accordingly, this Assessment does not conclude that "CPI is safe." Instead, it distinguishes **Risk pathways that have been reduced by design** from **uncertainties that still require evidence**. Those gaps directly define the next Safety activities: targeted Wet Lab experiments, quantitative modeling, product-quality characterization, expert consultation, and regulatory review. Reapplying PSAF whenever the recognition element, payload, target, manufacturing process, or Intended Use changes provides a consistent mechanism for carrying Safety assessment from research toward future implementation.
